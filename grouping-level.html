Why the grouping level changes the price effect in a price-volume-mix bridge

The grouping level is what a bridge treats as one product: a lens design, or a design with one coating, one color and one material. The price effect depends on that choice. This page shows why, with two bridges run on the same sales data.

What the price effect measures

A price-volume-mix analysis compares each product with the same product in the prior period. The price effect is current units times the change in average price, q1 × (p1 − p0). Product means whatever you group by, and the two prices are averages of that group. A coarse group blends every option sold under it.

One lens design is sold with a standard coating and a dearer coating. Neither price changes. This year more buyers choose the dearer coating, so the average price of the design rises. Grouped by design, the bridge sees one product whose price went up and reports a price effect. Grouped by design and coating, each coating is compared with itself, neither price changed, there is no price effect, and the shift is reported as mix. The total change in sales is the same either way. Only the split between the lines moves.

Two bridges on three million sales lines

The data is made up. It is prescription lens sales from the generator behind the tool's sample. Between the two years it contains a price increase on some designs, some new designs, a retired coating, and a shift toward photochromic lenses. What the full bridge reports on those lines, and on a configuration that is new or discontinued inside a design that sold in both years, the design-only bridge reports as price. The design-only price effect is the larger of the two. The shift toward photochromic lenses is in the Mix: Color line of the full bridge. In the design-only bridge it sits inside the price effect.

Why not group as finely as possible

A full configuration that sold in only one of the two years has no earlier price to compare with. Its sales cannot be a price effect. They go to New configurations or Discontinued configurations. In both tables, the new-configurations line is larger by full configuration than by design only, and the discontinued line is zero by design only. A coarse grouping moves mix into price, and a fine grouping moves sales out of price into new and discontinued. Neither grouping level avoids both.

Bridgekit compares each configuration with the same configuration in the prior period. Where there is no match, it falls back to the nearest group that did sell. See how Bridgekit handles this.

Check this on the Setup check tab

The tool reruns the bridge with each attribute left out in turn and lists the results on the Setup check tab. With the sample, the first row is Your setup. That row is the full-configuration bridge on one million sales lines, the same grouping as the first column of the second table. The rows under it are Without Design, Without Coating, Without Color, and Without Material. The vs your setup column shows how far the price effect moves each time. Matched exactly shows how much of current sales had the same configuration in the prior year. The tab has no design-only row. Leaving out one attribute is a smaller step than grouping by design alone.

On your own file, an attribute whose removal moves price a lot carries real mix, so keep it. One that barely moves price and lowers the match rate can be dropped.
