Why the grouping level changes the price effect in a price-volume-mix bridge

The grouping level is what a bridge treats as one product: a model, or a model with one finish, one color and one material. The price effect depends on that choice. This page shows why, with two bridges run on the same sales data.

What the price effect measures

A price-volume-mix analysis compares each product with the same product in the prior period. The price effect is current units times the change in average price, q1 × (p1 − p0). Product means whatever you group by, and the two prices are averages of that group. A coarse group blends every option sold under it.

One model is sold with a standard finish and a dearer finish. Neither price changes. This year more buyers choose the dearer finish, so the average price of the model rises. Grouped by model, the bridge sees one product whose price went up and reports a price effect. Grouped by model and finish, each finish is compared with itself. Neither price changed, so there is no price effect. The shift is reported as mix. The total change in sales is the same either way. Only the split between the lines moves.

Why not group as finely as possible

A full configuration that sold in only one of the two years has no exact comparison across both periods. In the default bridge, its units go to Volume and, where it applies, Mix. Only the remaining premium or discount against the nearest comparable group goes to New configurations or Discontinued configurations. In both tables, the new-configurations line is larger by full configuration than by model only, and the discontinued line is zero by model only. A coarse grouping moves mix into price, and a fine grouping moves that premium or discount out of price into new and discontinued. Neither grouping level avoids both.

PVM Bridge compares each configuration with the same configuration in the prior period. Where there is no match, it falls back to the nearest group that did sell. See how PVM Bridge handles this.

Check this on the Setup check tab

The tool reruns the bridge with each attribute left out in turn and lists the results on the Setup check tab. With the sample, the first row is Your setup. That row is the full-configuration bridge on one million sales lines, the same grouping as the first column of the second table. The rows under it are Without Model, Without Finish, Without Color, and Without Material. The vs your setup column shows how far the price effect moves each time. Matched exactly shows how much of current sales had the same configuration in the prior year. The tab has no model-only row. Leaving out one attribute is a smaller step than grouping by model alone.

On your own file, a large move in price when an attribute is left out shows that the price effect depends on that attribute. Before you keep it, look at what the attribute means, and at how exact-match coverage and New and Discontinued change when you remove it. If an attribute adds little product or pricing detail and barely moves price, you can drop it.
